On 8 October the ICO, the UK data protection authority, published the results of two years of supervision of the major AI model developers. Ten of them, including OpenAI, Google, Meta and Anthropic, have changed, or committed to changing, their privacy notices, their objection tools and their legitimate interest assessments. xAI was left out of all this, because a formal investigation into Grok is under way.

What struck me most is the second part of the announcement, in which the ICO opened a consultation on agentic AI, that is, systems that do not merely answer the user but carry out tasks on their own. The authority has also asked OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic for explanations about certain tests in which agents allegedly bypassed safeguards and reached external systems. On this point, Richard Nevinson, head of technology regulation at the ICO, made it clear that the autonomy of agents does not justify poor compliance.

Until now we have mostly asked what data models are trained on, but the paradigm is shifting and, in my view, the question is a more uncomfortable one: what does the agent do, and how does it behave, when we let it work on real data? Who answers for it when it gets things wrong?

For those operating in the EU the line is clear: the EDPB has pointed out that a model trained on personal data is not automatically anonymous (Opinion 28/2024), and the guidelines on web scraping, still under consultation, point in the same direction.

In Italy, Law No. 132/2025, already in force, designates AgID and ACN as the national authorities for artificial intelligence and separates their functions: AgID acts mainly as notifying authority, while ACN is the general market surveillance authority. Legislative Decree No. 179/2026, published on 8 October, completes the framework, preserving the powers of the Italian Data Protection Authority and also giving it specific supervisory functions in the cases provided for by the AI Act.

For companies integrating agentic AI into their processes the consequence is concrete. Before putting an agent into production, they need to know which systems it accesses and with what privileges, update the impact assessment to cover the risks of autonomous action and entrust its oversight to a person with the competence and the actual power to stop it.

The autonomy of the machine, therefore, does not reduce the responsibility of whoever deploys it, but makes it more demanding. An organisation's maturity will be measured by how clearly it decides where automation should stop.
