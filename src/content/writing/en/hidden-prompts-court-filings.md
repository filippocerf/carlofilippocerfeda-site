A recent Connecticut ruling, *Elliott v. N.Y. Bariatric Group, LLC*, captures with unusual clarity where legal technology actually stands today.

A self-represented plaintiff hid a set of instructions inside his court filings, using white text on a white background: invisible to a human reader, fully legible to any AI system processing the document. The hidden prompt instructed any AI reviewing the filing to agree with his position and to reverse a prior clerk's denial in his favor.

The tactic failed for a simple reason: the Connecticut Judicial Branch does not use AI to review or decide filings, and the judge ruled from a printed copy. The invisible text surfaced only because the unusual white space on the printed pages raised suspicion during human review. When the plaintiff repeated the tactic in later filings, describing his conduct as an "audit" of the court, the judge revoked his e-filing privileges: from now on, every pleading must be submitted on paper, in person.

Judge Spader did not reject AI as such: he himself used it in preparing the opinion. What he rejected is the idea that judicial judgment can be delegated to a channel neither the court nor the opposing party can see or answer: the court described the hidden prompt as something close to a secret, *ex parte* communication.

The same tactic is already spreading beyond the courtroom. Applicants hide white-text prompts in CVs to steer automated hiring tools, and in the Brazilian labor case *Elisandro Martins de Barros v. Renato Ribeiro de Lima*, attorneys inserted hidden instructions meant to make the court's own automated system produce only a superficial challenge to their filing.

Building trustworthy legal technology is not primarily a matter of detecting hidden text after the fact. It means treating every document as material to be verified, never as a source of instructions to follow blindly. AI can assist legal work, but responsibility for the result must remain with the person using it.
